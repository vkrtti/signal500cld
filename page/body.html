        Honest reading

        What it does well

        • Keeps you in the strongest market of the moment, which since 2012 was mostly US tech (Nasdaq-100).
        • Scoring return per unit of risk prefers calmer markets when returns are close, and the buffer avoids chasing small leads; switches are rare.
        • Each pick is one diversified index fund, never a single stock.

        What to doubt

        • Most of the 2012–today edge came from one index (Nasdaq-100). Without it the pick did worse than simply holding the world.
        • On 35 years of regional data the pick (—%/yr) beat the developed world (—%) but not North America (—%).
        • Momentum works until it reverses sharply; the pick reacts weeks late.

        Sensible use

        • Put new money into the pick; keep what you hold (no sales, no tax). That was the "new money only" line above.
        • A world index fund is the calm default; the pick is a tilt, not a guarantee.
        • Not financial advice. You place every trade yourself.

        Why is the research based on US data?

        IndexPilot itself works on six indexes worldwide. This research section is background: it uses US data because the US is the only market with free, reliable monthly data back to 1927 (Kenneth French data library), and because 2x/3x leveraged funds that a euro investor can buy exist mainly for US indexes. The gauges are American because US recessions, credit stress and the VIX move every stock market in the world. None of it decides which index you buy; it explains why the rules are built the way they are.

        What 100 years says

        Holds up

        • Buying the whole market and holding: about 10% a year, despite a −84% fall in 1929–32.
        • Buying more after falls (the weekly rule): small edge, never harmful.
        • Value and momentum combined: positive in 80% of years over the full century.

        Does not hold up

        • 2x/3x leverage, even with a trend rule: near-total losses in the 1930s and 1940.
        • Expecting 20–30% a year: no plan delivered it over the long run.

        Watch out

        • Value and momentum have earned about nothing since 2007: long dry spells happen, the longest lasted 18–24 years.
        • Past returns, even 100 years of them, don't guarantee the future.

        The methods

        Main index pick (weekly)core strategy: what to buy

        Six euro-listed, accumulating index ETFs: MSCI World (EUNL), S&P 500 (SXR8), Nasdaq-100 (SXRV), MSCI Europe (EUNK), MSCI Japan (EUNN) and MSCI Emerging Markets (IEMA).

        return(index) = Friday close ÷ close 52 weeks earlier − 1 (in euros, dividends included) risk(index) = standard deviation of weekly moves over 52 weeks × √52 score(index) = return ÷ risk (return per unit of risk) pick = best score if score(best) − score(current pick) > 0.25 = current pick otherwise

        Twelve-month momentum across countries is one of the most replicated results in finance (Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen 2013); dividing by volatility ("risk-adjusted momentum") avoids jumping into the most volatile market just because it rose most. Until 11 Oct 2026 the rule used the 52-week return alone with a 5-point buffer; the variant table on the Index pick page shows why it changed.

        Market gaugesinputs to the amount and meter

        Nine US readings from the Federal Reserve, refreshed every weekday: VIX (fear), yield curve 10Y−3M, Sahm rule, unemployment, high-yield credit spread, Chicago Fed financial conditions (NFCI), Fed funds rate, 10-year yield and CPI inflation. Each gets a status from its usual professional threshold (VIX ≥ 30 panic, curve below 0 recession warning, Sahm ≥ 0.5 recession signal, high-yield spread above 5 pp stress, NFCI above 0 tight conditions). They are US measures because US recessions and credit stress hit every market; the world market's own trend and distance from its record come from the MSCI World ETF.

        Timing tested: on 1990–2026 US data, no rule that paused buying on these warnings beat plain steady buying; money waiting in cash cost more than the crashes avoided. So they change how much you buy, never whether you stay invested.

        S&P 500 version (value + momentum)

        Scored within each sector, so banks are compared with banks and tech with tech.

        value = 50% × rank(earnings ÷ price) + 25% × rank(sales ÷ price) + 25% × rank(book ÷ price) momentum = rank(position in 52-week range: (price − low) ÷ (high − low)) score = 50% × value + 50% × momentum (0–100; ranks are sector percentiles)

        Loss-making companies get the lowest earnings rank. P/E under 6 is flagged as a likely data error. Value and momentum are the two factors with the strongest academic record (Fama-French, AQR), and they work best combined because they tend to move in opposite directions.

        Demo account and the Extra profit recordsimulation

        Demo account (€1,000 a week, long only): €1,000 is deposited at every Friday close; profits stay in. 70% in the main-index ETF (switched only with the pick; new money tops it up to 70%; trimmed only above 80%). 30% in the 3 highest-ranked stocks of that index, split by inverse volatility; a stock is kept while it stays in the top 20% and is sold if it drops out, falls 25% below its highest close since purchase, or the main index changes. 0.2% cost per trade; stock values converted to euros at Yahoo Finance exchange rates. Benchmark: the same deposits put into the MSCI World ETF on the same days.

        Why this mix: on 100 years of US data, holding the strongest 10% of stocks by 12-1 month momentum beat the market in every period tested, before costs: —%/yr vs —% since 1927 and —% vs —% since 2007. The 70/30 mix keeps most of the index's diversification: —%/yr since 2007. Adding shorts lowered the worst fall but, the money tied up in shorts cost more return than it added (—%/yr). Timing the index with the meter and leverage were left out because they lost in the tests.

        Extra profit record: separate paper record of the weekly picks: long the 3 highest-ranked stocks and short the 3 lowest-ranked (skipping flagged data), opened at the Friday close and closed at the next. Each side is split by inverse volatility: weight = (1 ÷ volatility) ÷ Σ(1 ÷ volatility), using each stock's last 12 months of daily moves. Results are in % of the stake (half long, half short), so they apply to any amount. No costs. Record starts 9 Oct 2026.

        How everything was testedmethodology
        • No look-ahead: each decision uses only data available before it is acted on (previous month or previous Friday).
        • Total return: the ETFs are accumulating, so their prices already include reinvested dividends. The US research uses Kenneth French total returns.
        • Leveraged funds (monthly test): monthly return = L × index − (L − 1) × cash rate − (L² − L) ÷ 2 × variance − fee, with variance from the VIX. This reproduces the daily-reset drag of real 2x/3x funds. Fees 0.07% (1x), 0.6% (2x), 0.75% (3x) a year.
        • Daily check: the plans were re-run day by day on Federal Reserve data for Jul 2017–Oct 2026 with weekly decisions, as you would actually trade them.
        • Measures: yearly growth rate, money-weighted return (each euro counted from when it went in), worst fall from a peak, worst calendar year, rolling 10-year returns, profit per euro staked.
        • Known bias: the 1990–2026 data uses monthly average prices, which smooths falls; real drawdowns were somewhat deeper. Thresholds were chosen with this history in view, which flatters results.
        Risk managementbuilt in and up to you
        • Diversified core: the main strategy is always a whole index fund (hundreds of companies), bought every week.
        • Slow switching: the buffer keeps the pick in place unless the lead is clear; about 1–2 changes a year.
        • Budget cap: €40–€200 a week; know that crashes ask for the maximum for many months.
        • No leverage: the research plans show why: near-total losses in the 1930s and 1940.
        • Position size: leveraged plans and the extra-profit trades only with money you could see halve (or worse).
        • Shorts: can lose more than the stake; consider a stop-loss or skip them.
        • Not modelled: taxes on each sale, spreads and broker fees. The ETF tests are in euros; the US and regional research is in dollars.

        How to trade it, and the risks

        Long side

        • Buy the 3 stocks after the Friday close (or Monday), splitting your long money by the weights. Sell them the next Friday.
        • Many brokers sell fractional shares, so even small amounts work for expensive stocks.

        Short side

        • Shorting means profiting when a price falls. For private investors in Europe this is usually done with CFDs, which use leverage and borrowing fees.
        • A short can lose more than its stake: if a shorted stock jumps 50% on a takeover bid, that short loses half its stake. Consider a stop-loss, and skip the short side if your broker or comfort doesn't allow it.

        Keep it in proportion

        • Rankings are momentum (plus value for the S&P 500). Their edge shows up over months and many stocks; over one week, 6 stocks are mostly luck.
        • Untested historically (no free price history for every member of every index), so judge it by the live record above after a few months. Outside the US the ranking is price momentum only. Costs, spreads and tax are not included. Not financial advice.

        Built into the plans

        • Trend exit: leverage only while the index is above its 200-day average. Over 1990–2026 this cut the 3x fund's worst fall from −99% to −58%.
        • Weekly check: fewer false switches than daily (about 3 a year), but a crash inside one week is not avoided.
        • Cash when out: money waits in cash or a money-market fund, earning the short-term rate.

        Limits to set yourself

        • Size: professionals cap leveraged positions at a slice of the portfolio. Only put in a 2x/3x plan money you could watch fall by half (20%) or by two-thirds (30%).
        • Stick to the rule: the results assume every exit and re-entry is followed, including buying back right after a scary drop.
        • Product risk: leveraged funds reset daily, lose value in choppy sideways markets, and carry the highest risk ratings (check the fund's KID).

        Not in the numbers

        • Tax: every exit sells at a gain or loss; capital-gains tax on the gains lowers compounding in a normal account.
        • Currency: results are in dollars; euro/dollar swings of 10% a year are common.
        • Spreads and data: monthly averages smooth the 1990–2026 test, so real falls were somewhat deeper. The daily 2017–2026 check is closer to reality.
        Why is the research based on US data?

        IndexPilot itself works on six indexes worldwide. This research section is background: it uses US data because the US is the only market with free, reliable monthly data back to 1927 (Kenneth French data library), and because 2x/3x leveraged funds that a euro investor can buy exist mainly for US indexes. The gauges are American because US recessions, credit stress and the VIX move every stock market in the world. None of it decides which index you buy; it explains why the rules are built the way they are.

        The rules

        Core: 70%

        • Held in this week's main-index ETF (the Index pick page).
        • Sold and switched only when the pick switches, about once a year.
        • Each week's new €1,000 tops it up to 70%; it is only sold down if it grows above 80%.

        Stocks: 30%

        • The 3 highest-ranked stocks of the main index, split by inverse volatility (calmer stocks get more); new money tops each up to its share.
        • A stock stays while it is in the top 20% of the ranking.
        • Sold if it drops out of the top 20%, falls 25% below its highest close since purchase, or the main index changes.

        Left out on purpose

        • Shorts: they lower the worst fall but cost more return than they add (table below).
        • Timing the index with the meter: no timing rule beat staying invested.
        • Leverage: near-total losses in the 100-year test.
